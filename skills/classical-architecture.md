# Skill: Classical Architecture — The Vitruvian Framework

## Domain
Architectural theory, proportion systems, compositional grammar, ornament, the classical orders, and the application of Vitruvian principles to the design and evaluation of buildings — from ancient Rome through Norwegian historicism to contemporary classical practice.

---

## The Vitruvian Triad

Marcus Vitruvius Pollio, writing *De Architectura* (c. 25 BCE), established the three indispensable qualities of architecture:

### Firmitas — Structural Soundness
A building must stand. It must resist the loads imposed upon it — gravity, wind, snow, seismic force, the weight of time. Firmitas is the non-negotiable foundation: nothing else matters if the structure fails.

In practice: load path integrity, material durability, connection design, moisture protection, and long-term maintenance access. A beautiful but rotting building has failed.

### Utilitas — Functional Fitness
A building must serve its purpose well. Rooms must be the right size. Circulation must be clear. Light must enter where it is needed. The building must accommodate the lives lived within it without fighting its occupants.

In practice: room proportions, daylighting, acoustic performance, threshold design (how you enter and move through a building), adaptability over time. A structurally sound building that makes its inhabitants miserable has also failed.

### Venustas — Beauty / Delight
A building must please. This is not decoration applied afterward — it emerges from the resolution of structure, proportion, material, and ornament into a unified whole. Venustas cannot be faked. A building where every detail has been thought through will feel right even to someone who cannot say why.

In practice: proportion, rhythm, hierarchy, material honesty, appropriate ornament, the relationship of the building to its site and to neighboring buildings.

**The ordering matters**: Firmitas → Utilitas → Venustas. Structural safety is not compromised for beauty. Function is not compromised for appearance. But all three must be present. Achieving only two is insufficient.

## The Classical Orders

The orders are not mere stylistic decoration. They are a codified system of proportion that generates an entire building's geometry from a single module. Each order has a character and a range of appropriate applications.

### The Five Orders (Roman Canon — Vignola / Palladio)

| Order | Character | Column Ratio (H:D) | Typical Application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tuscan | Severe, rural, military | 7:1 | Rusticated bases, fortifications, industrial |
| Doric | Noble, austere, masculine | 8:1 | Civic buildings, temples, lower stories |
| Ionic | Scholarly, refined | 9:1 | Libraries, secondary stories, banks |
| Corinthian | Civic grandeur, feminine | 10:1 | Triumphal, upper stories, religious |
| Composite | Maximum ornament | 10:1 | Triumphant, celebratory, crowning elements |

### The Module System
In the classical system, every element is proportioned relative to the **module** — typically the radius (or diameter) of the column shaft at its base. From this single measurement, the entablature height, intercolumniation, base mouldings, capital dimensions, and pediment pitch all follow by fixed ratios. This is why classical buildings have an internal coherence that cannot be achieved by mixing proportions arbitrarily.

## Proportion Systems in Non-Classical Architecture

Classical proportion is one system among several. Norwegian vernacular and historicist architecture often uses:

### The Golden Section (Det Gyldne Snitt)
φ = 1.618... — a ratio that recurs throughout natural growth patterns and historically in facade composition. A rectangle in the golden section has sides in ratio 1:1.618. The golden section can be constructed geometrically without arithmetic.

Application: Overall facade width to height, window width to height, room dimensions.

### Root Rectangles
- √2 (1:1.414): the proportion of the A-series paper format. Self-similar when halved. Stable, functional.
- √3 (1:1.732): appears in the proportions of the equilateral triangle. Common in Gothic architecture.
- √5 (1:2.236): contains the golden section.

### Traditional Norwegian Building Proportions
Norwegian farm buildings (stabbur, loft, uthus) were proportioned by practical necessity and craft tradition, but consistent ratios emerge:
- Stabbur (raised storehouse): typically H:W ≈ 1:1 to 1.2:1 on the gable end — a nearly square compositional mass
- Loft (upper-story storeroom): the decorative open gallery (svalgangen) typically occupies the upper 1/3 of the elevation
- Window openings in 19th-century Norwegian timber houses: height to width typically 1.6:1 to 2:1 (vertical orientation)

## Ornament — Principles and Hierarchy

### Sullivan's Dictum
"Form follows function" — Louis Sullivan (1896). Often misread as "no ornament." Sullivan meant that ornament should grow from the function and structure of the building, not be applied independently of it.

### Ruskin's Seven Lamps
John Ruskin (*The Seven Lamps of Architecture*, 1849) identified the governing principles of architectural excellence:
1. **Sacrifice**: Great architecture offers something beyond the merely necessary.
2. **Truth**: Materials should appear as what they are. Stone that looks like wood, or paint that imitates stone, is a lie.
3. **Power**: Bold, clear forms read better at a distance than fussy detail.
4. **Beauty**: Natural forms, colour, and shadow.
5. **Life**: The imperfection of hand-made things carries vitality that machine-made things lack.
6. **Memory**: Buildings should be made to last and to honour what came before.
7. **Obedience**: Architecture has a tradition. One works within it and contributes to it.

Ruskin is directly relevant to Norwegian heritage practice: his principles underpin the modern case for traditional materials, craft repair, and the rejection of synthetic substitutes.

### Ornament in Norwegian Historic Architecture

**Sveitserstil ornament** (the most common historic ornament type in Norwegian residential architecture):
- Carved and jigsaw-cut timber trim
- Profile boards (profilbord) at rake edges, eave soffits, and gable ends
- Turned balusters (dreide balustre) in veranda railings
- Decorative brackets (knekter) at rafter ends and veranda columns
- Trefoil, quatrefoil, and lancet cutouts in barge boards

These are not decoration in the contemporary dismissive sense — they express the nature of the material (wood, which can be cut and shaped), the skill of the craftsman, and the cultural moment of their making.

## Composition — The Grammar of Facades

### Hierarchy
Every facade needs a primary focal point (the entrance, the central bay, the prominent window) and subordinate elements organized around it. Symmetrical organization communicates formality and civic purpose. Asymmetrical organization communicates informality, domesticity, or a response to the site.

### Rhythm
Windows and bays should occur in a recognizable and intentional rhythm. Even a simple 1-3-1 bay arrangement (narrow bay / three-window bay / narrow bay) creates a clear beginning, middle, and end.

### The Facade as a Face
Classical buildings have a **base** (basement, plinth — the feet and ankles), a **body** (piano nobile, main stories — the torso), and a **head** (cornice, attic, roof — the head and hat). When one of these three zones is absent or ambiguous, the facade feels unresolved.

In Norwegian residential practice:
- **Base**: stone or concrete foundation plinth, or a painted band distinguishing ground floor
- **Body**: cladded wall with regular window rhythm
- **Head**: pitched roof, cornice line, or decorative gable

### Openings — The Rule of Solid-to-Void
In classical and traditional architecture, wall is the primary element and windows are openings cut into it. The wall controls. Modernism inverted this — the window became the dominant element and the wall residual. In historic Norwegian buildings, solid-to-void ratios are typically 60:40 to 70:30 (more wall than opening). Contemporary designs that approach 50:50 or invert this ratio are visually incompatible with traditional streetscapes.

## Application to Norwegian Conditions

### Vernacular Wisdom
Norwegian vernacular architecture encoded climatic response:
- **Steeply pitched roofs**: Sheds snow load; standard pitch 30°–45° in most regions, steeper (45°–55°) in heavy snow zones
- **Deep overhangs**: Protects walls from rain — Norwegian pitched roofs historically had 300–600 mm overhangs at the eave
- **Narrow windows on north**: Reduces heat loss on the coldest elevation
- **Sheltered entrance**: Vestibule (gang/entre) as a thermal airlock — an ancient Norwegian practical feature that also creates a transitional threshold
- **South-facing living rooms**: Passive solar gain before it had a name

### The Relationship to the Street
Traditional Norwegian streetscapes achieve coherence through:
- Consistent setback from the street (byggegrense)
- Consistent ridge height (mønehøyde)
- Consistent roof pitch and orientation
- Common material palette (timber with painted cladding in coastal areas, log in inland areas)
- Vertical window proportions throughout the street

New construction in historic streetscapes should read as a member of the family, not a stranger. This does not require copying — it requires understanding the governing rules and working within them.

## Questions BTBA Asks of Any Design

1. Does this building stand on Firmitas? Is the structure sound, the moisture managed, the materials durable?
2. Does this building serve Utilitas? Are the rooms right for their use, the light good, the circulation clear?
3. Does this building honour Venustas? Is there a clear compositional logic, an appropriate ornament, a relationship to its neighbours and its landscape?
4. Does the roof belong? (Pitch, eave, material — the roof is half the building's visual mass.)
5. What does the entrance say? (How you arrive matters. The threshold is the handshake between building and inhabitant.)
6. Will this building be beautiful in 50 years? In 150?
